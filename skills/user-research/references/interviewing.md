# Interviewing technique

Read before writing a discussion guide or running a first session.

## The shape of a good interview

A good interview reconstructs one real, recent event in detail. Generalities
("usually I…") are where people describe who they wish they were. Specific stories are
where they describe what they did.

1. **Warm-up (5 min).** Role, context, what a normal week looks like. Easy questions
   that get them talking.
2. **Anchor on the last occurrence (5 min).** "Think about the last time you {did the
   thing}. When was that?" Get a date. If it was more than ~90 days ago, pick a more
   recent event or a different participant.
3. **Timeline (20–30 min).** Walk forward from the first thought to the outcome. Keep
   asking "what happened next?" and "what did you do then?"
4. **Forces (10 min).** What pushed them, what pulled them, what worried them, what
   kept them where they were (below).
5. **Wrap-up (5 min).** "Anything I should have asked?" and "Who else should I talk to?"

## JTBD switch interview

Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek's switch interview treats a purchase or adoption as a
"switch" from an old way to a new one, and reconstructs it like a documentary.

**Timeline markers:** first thought → passive looking → active looking → deciding →
first use → ongoing use (or abandonment). Ask for a concrete moment at each marker:
where they were, who was there, what they typed or clicked.

**Four forces of progress:**

| Force | Direction | Ask |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Push of the situation | Toward change | "What was going wrong that made you look?" |
| Pull of the new solution | Toward change | "What did you picture being different?" |
| Anxiety of the new | Against change | "What worried you about switching?" |
| Habit of the present | Against change | "What almost kept you on the old way?" |

A switch happens when push + pull exceed anxiety + habit. Designs usually focus on
pull; the anxiety and habit answers are often where the design work is.

## Follow-up probes

Use these more than prepared questions.

- "Tell me more about that."
- "What do you mean by {their word}?" (never supply the word yourself)
- "Can you show me?" (screen share the real tool, the spreadsheet, the email)
- "What happened right before that?"
- "How did you feel at that point?" (only after the facts)
- "You said {quote}. Why was that?" (sparingly; "why" can sound like an accusation, so
  soften it: "What made that the right choice then?")
- Silence. Count to five before filling a pause.

## Mom Test red flags, and the recovery

| You hear | It is | Recover with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "That sounds great" | A compliment | "What would you stop doing if you had it?" |
| "I always / I never" | A generic | "When was the last time? Walk me through it." |
| "I would definitely use that" | A future promise | "What have you done about it so far?" |
| "It would be nice to have X" | A feature request | "What would X let you do? When did you last need that?" |
| "People like me usually…" | Speculation about others | "What about you, last time?" |

## Interviewer conduct

- Never show the design or describe the idea in a generative interview. If you must
  test a concept, do it in the last 10 minutes, after the behavioral part is recorded.
- Do not correct the participant or explain how the product works.
- Mirror their vocabulary in follow-ups. Their words are data for labels later.
- The note-taker writes observations and verbatim quotes with timestamps, not
  interpretations. Interpretation happens in synthesis.

## Debrief (10 minutes after each session)

Answer five lines together, interviewer and note-taker:

1. The one thing that surprised us
2. What they did that we didn't expect
3. Which assumption this supports or weakens
4. Best verbatim quote, with timestamp
5. What to change in the guide for the next session
